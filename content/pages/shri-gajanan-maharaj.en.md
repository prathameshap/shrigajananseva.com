On Magh Shukla Saptami in Shaka 1800 — February 1878 — a young sanyasi was seen for the first time in Shegaon, a small town in the Vidarbha region of Maharashtra.

There had been a feast. The leaf-plates had been thrown on a heap outside, and he was sitting beside them, eating the grains of rice left on them and drinking water from a puddle in the road. Two men, Bankatlal and Damodar, stopped and asked him why a man would do such a thing.

He said the grain had been grown by somebody's labour, and it was not his to waste.

Nobody ever established where he had come from, what his given name was, or how old he was. He was called Gajanan because of the six syllables he said constantly — *Gan Gan Ganat Bote* — and Shegaon is where he stayed for the thirty-two years that followed.

## What he was like

The accounts, gathered by Dasganu Maharaj in the **Shri Gajanan Vijay Grantha**, describe someone almost entirely unconcerned with how he appeared. He wore next to nothing. He sat where he liked, ate what was in front of him or nothing at all, and slept in the open. He accepted no honours, refused to be installed anywhere, and took no disciples in any formal sense.

He did not preach. There are no discourses to read, no system to study, and no lineage of initiation to trace. When people arrived in trouble, he usually said something short and often something that sounded like nothing at all, and they went away changed. When people arrived wanting to be impressed, he generally ignored them.

Miracles are recorded in the Grantha — water drawn from dry ground, a fire that did not burn, illnesses that lifted. They are recorded, in the tradition's own telling, as beside the point. The point was that he behaved the same way towards a landowner and towards a beggar, and never once made a distinction between them.

## The practice he left

**Gan Gan Ganat Bote.**

That is it. He repeated those six syllables throughout his life and gave no other method. He set no prerequisite, demanded no initiation, charged nothing, appointed no intermediary and specified no correct time, posture, language or degree of purity.

This matters more than it first sounds. A practice with no gate cannot be gatekept. Anybody can begin it, now, from wherever they are and in whatever state they are in, and nobody can tell them they are doing it wrong or that they are not yet ready. That openness is the whole inheritance, and it is the reason this centre asks nothing of anyone who walks in.

## Samadhi

In the days before Rishi Panchami in 1910, he told the devotees around him plainly that he was going to leave the body, and equally plainly that he was not going anywhere.

He took samadhi on Rishi Panchami — the fifth day of the bright fortnight of Bhadrapad — in Shegaon, where the samadhi mandir stands and where lakhs of people still come every year.

The three days the sangha keeps are these: **Pragat Din**, the day he first appeared; **Guru Pournima**, when a disciple thanks the guru; and **Rishi Panchami**, his punyatithi. All three follow the tithi rather than the Gregorian calendar, which is why the dates on [our occasions page](/occasions) move each year.

## Reading further

The **Shri Gajanan Vijay Grantha**, composed by Dasganu Maharaj in twenty-one adhyays, is the source for everything above. The centre reads it in full across the seven days before Pragat Din, and one adhyay at a time on ordinary Thursdays.

It is in the [library](/library/gajanan-vijay-grantha), free to download, as is everything else we publish.

On 23 February 1878, in the town of Shegaon in Maharashtra, two local gentlemen came upon a young man by the roadside.

He was, in the words handed down to us, "a youth, who was almost all bare with radiant face, picking up particles from leftover food" that had been thrown out after a meal. He drank water left for cattle. To anyone watching he would have looked deranged. The two men looked again, and saw something else.

That day is observed as Pragat Din — the day Maharaj became visible to the world. He was not born in Shegaon and gave no account of where he came from. He simply appeared, fully formed, indifferent to the categories by which people are usually sorted.

## Thirty-two years

Maharaj remained in Shegaon for thirty-two years.

In that time an extraordinary number of accounts accumulated around him. He healed leprosy. He revived a dog that had died. He sat calmly in the middle of a fire and was untouched by it. He pacified a horse no one else could approach. Devotees came with every kind of difficulty — illness, debt, grief, doubt — and left steadied.

The miracles are the part of the story most easily told, and the part Maharaj himself cared least about. They were never performed for display. They happened around him the way weather happens around a mountain.

## What he taught

> No being is separated from God; God exists in all and all exist in God.

This was the centre of it. Maharaj taught that God is present in each and every person and in all things around us, and that the only honest response to this is to treat every being — without exception, without ranking — as equal and deserving of respect.

The rest followed from it:

- **He had no patience for pretense.** Outward show of piety without inward truth he simply ignored.
- **He named ego as the great obstacle.** Not desire, not ignorance — ego. The conviction of being separate and important is what stands between a person and what is already there.
- **He asked for humility, good deeds, devotion and righteousness.** Four ordinary things, available to anyone, requiring no learning and no lineage.

He gave no elaborate system. To those who asked him for a practice he gave a nam — **Gan Gan Ganat Bote** — and that was sufficient.

## Samadhi

Maharaj took Samadhi on 8 September 1910, on the day of Rishi Panchami. He chose the day himself.

Devotees mark the anniversary not as a bereavement but as a completion: a life that had been given away entirely, finishing exactly when and how it was meant to.

## Shri Gajanan Vijay Grantha

The life of Maharaj was set down by Saint Dasaganu Maharaj — a disciple of Shri Sai Baba of Shirdi, and a poet of real gift — in the **Shri Gajanan Vijay Grantha**, a devotional text of twenty-one chapters.

It follows Maharaj from his arrival in Shegaon through to his Samadhi. It is the text devotees read during parayan, and the reason the accounts above have survived in the form they have.

The Grantha is available in our library, along with the stotras and aartis recited daily at the mandir.
